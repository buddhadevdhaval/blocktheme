How genetic testing is changing the future of personalized medicine

Posted by Dr. Sarah Collins
Share:
Reading time: 7 mins

The promise of personalized medicine—tailoring treatments to individual patients based on their unique genetic makeup—is rapidly becoming a clinical reality. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels have made it possible to simultaneously analyze hundreds of genes with a single blood or saliva sample, giving providers unprecedented insight into inherited risk.

Why hereditary testing matters

Approximately 5–10% of all cancers are caused by inherited gene mutations passed from parent to child. When a pathogenic variant is identified in a patient, at-risk family members can be offered targeted testing to determine whether they carry the same mutation. Early detection enables proactive surveillance, risk-reduction strategies, and—in some cases—prophylactic interventions that dramatically improve outcomes.

Beyond oncology, inherited variants linked to cardiomyopathies, arrhythmia syndromes, and metabolic disorders increasingly inform clinical decision-making across specialties. A cardiologist who knows a patient carries a pathogenic MYBPC3 variant, for instance, can adjust the frequency and modality of their monitoring long before symptoms appear.

How NGS panels have evolved

Early genetic testing focused on single-gene or small multi-gene panels. Today's comprehensive panels integrate whole-exome and targeted deep-sequencing approaches that cover hundreds of clinically validated genes in a single workflow. Advances in bioinformatics—particularly machine-learning-based variant calling—have simultaneously increased sensitivity and reduced false-positive rates.

Ambry's AmbryCORE™ bioinformatics pipeline integrates population-level data from internal databases, published literature, and functional assay results to generate evidence-based variant classifications. This means patients receive more accurate, actionable reports—and fewer variants of uncertain significance (VUS) remain in that uncertain category over time.

What this means for providers and patients

For clinicians, the expanding evidence base around hereditary conditions translates to clearer ordering criteria and more confident post-result counseling. For patients, it means the opportunity to move from uncertainty to informed action—whether that's enrolling in a surveillance program, discussing risk-reduction surgery, or simply knowing that a negative result provides reassurance.

The conversation around genetic testing is also shifting. Patients are increasingly arriving in clinics having done their own research, armed with direct-to-consumer results and a list of questions. Providers who understand the landscape—and who partner with a laboratory committed to ongoing education and support—are best positioned to guide those conversations effectively.

Looking ahead

The integration of genomic data with electronic health records, real-world evidence platforms, and AI-assisted clinical decision support tools will further accelerate the translation of genetic insights into routine care. As variant databases grow and reclassification rates improve, patients with current VUS results will increasingly receive definitive reclassifications—a critical reason why choosing a laboratory committed to lifelong result support matters as much as technical accuracy.
