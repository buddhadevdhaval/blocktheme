Who Should Consider Testing?

Situations where genetic testing may be helpful

Unlike some conditions with clear risk checklists, neurological disorders don't follow a single pattern. Instead, testing is helpful in a range of situations. Select those that apply to your family.
Unexplained neurological symptoms
A family member has seizures, developmental delays, movement disorders, or other neurological symptoms without clear diagnosis
Child with developmental delay or autism
A child is experiencing developmental regression, intellectual disability, or has received an autism spectrum disorder diagnosis
Family history of neurological disorder
Multiple family members across generations have been diagnosed with a similar neurological condition
Seeking clarity on treatment options
Knowing the specific gene involved could help guide medication choice, dietary therapy, or other management decisions
Family planning considerations
You or a partner carry a known neurological gene variant, or have had a child with a disorder and want to understand recurrence risk
Avoiding additional invasive testing
Genetic results may help confirm or rule out a diagnosis without requiring additional biopsies, imaging, or other invasive procedures

Understanding Results

What your result actually means

Results from hereditary cancer genetic testing fall into one of three categories. Here's what each one means in plain language.

Negative

No cancer-linked genetic mutations were found in any of your tested genes.

A negative result is reassuring, but since risk is also based on personal and family history, discuss next steps with your provider.

Depending on their clinical history, genetic testing may still be advisable for family members.

Positive

A mutation linked to hereditary cancer was found in at least one of your tested genes.

A positive result confirms an increased cancer risk and may be the basis for specific medical management recommendations tied to that gene.

There may be up to a 50/50 chance that your close family members carry the same mutation. Testing for them may be recommended.

VUS

A mutation was found, but it's currently unclear if it increases cancer risk.

VUS = Variant of Unknown Significance. Management should be based on personal and family history until more is known.

These results can change over time as science advances — Ambry's Patient for Life program may notify you of updates.
